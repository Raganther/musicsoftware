# 2026-08-20 — groove: it takes your timing and leaves your notes

**Sketches touched:** `sketches/groove`
**Settings worth keeping:** seed 9, dorian, feel 1, busy 0.42, learns 0.25 —
play sixteenths deliberately behind the beat for a few bars and then stop, and
the partner keeps your drag while playing something you did not. The
demonstration is the three-way switch: `Feel` at 0 is a machine sitting next to
you, at 1 it is you, at 1.5 it is a caricature of you. `Against you` is the one
worth keeping for actual playing — it is a second player with the opposite
habit, and it is much harder to stay upright against than the copy is.

## What I tried

Improvisation/performance, the stalest family (last visited 2026-08-15,
conduct).

Every accompanist in this repo copies what you played. `call-response` answers
your phrase; `understudy` predicts your next note. All of them treat the notes
as the content. But most of what makes a player recognisable is not the notes,
it is where they put them — a drummer with your swing playing a different
pattern sounds more like you than one playing your pattern straight.

So: an accompanist that discards your pitches entirely. It keeps one running
mean of your deviation from the grid per position in the bar — a groove
template, the same object an MPC extracts — and places its own invented line on
it. `Feel` scales the template, so 0 is quantised and 1.5 exaggerates, and
`Against you` mirrors it.

The scheduling detail worth recording: a partner note can land *before* the
grid, and you cannot schedule into the past, so every partner note is decided a
whole step early and scheduled at (next step + offset). The clock's ~120 ms
lookahead is what makes a negative offset possible at all.

## What I measured

A human is the wrong instrument for this test, so the harness played: a known
groove of −25, 0, +55, 0 ms per sixteenth, fourteen bars, dispatched as real
key events at grid + deviation. Three questions, deliberately kept apart.

**Does it learn?** Template against injection: slope **1.004**, r **0.997**,
all sixteen positions.

**Does it schedule on it?** From the model:

| | slope | r |
| --- | --- | --- |
| feel 1.0 | 0.943 | 0.996 |
| feel 0 | **0.000** | — |
| feel 1.5 | 1.164 | 0.993 |
| against you | **−0.939** | −0.994 |

**Does the audio come out there?** Onsets from the spectrum, the two voices
three filter poles apart:

| | slope | r |
| --- | --- | --- |
| feel 1.0 | 0.774 | **0.986** |
| feel 0 | −0.055 | −0.312 |
| feel 1.5 | 0.916 | 0.969 |
| against you | −0.844 | **−0.969** |

Both controls behave: nothing at feel 0, cleanly inverted when mirrored.
Pre-limiter 0.465–0.512 played densely, 0.194 in the suite. Green: 27 sketches
+ jam, no clipping, clean teardown.

## The measurement was the hard part, and it lied for an hour

The first three runs said the partner was not playing the groove at all —
slope 0.014, r 0.093. The template was perfect and the audio showed nothing.

What saved it was a number I nearly did not print: **my own hits, measured the
same way, also read zero**. Those are ground truth — the harness dispatched
them at known times — so a detector that cannot see the groove in them is not
measuring the sketch, it is measuring itself. That one line turned a false
negative about the instrument into a bug report about the harness.

Three faults, found in order:

1. A two-second reverb tail sitting on top of every onset. Same lesson as
   yesterday's plate: added a `Room` parameter and set it to 0 to measure.
2. A per-buffer adaptive threshold, so a decaying note kept re-arming its own
   detector. Replaced with a slowly-decaying global reference and a
   fall-back-below requirement.
3. The actual killer: **the notes were longer than the gaps between them.** At
   340 ms on a 156 ms grid they overlapped, the envelope dipped and re-armed
   mid-note, and the detector reported 416 onsets where 224 existed. Roughly
   half of everything it found was invented, scattered uniformly, which pulls
   every per-slot average toward zero — exactly the symptom, and exactly the
   wrong conclusion.

Shortening the notes to 190 ms took the detector from 416 onsets and ±20.9 ms
of timestamp scatter to 292 onsets and ±3.0 ms, and every correlation went from
~0.1 to >0.96 in one step.

I also learned something about `AnalyserNode` worth writing down: it hands you
samples but no trustworthy timestamp for them. `currentTime` read alongside
`getFloatTimeDomainData` is off by something on the order of the output
latency, and it varies. Anchoring onsets to the buffer end is fine for
*relative* comparisons across many hits and useless for absolute ones.

## The recurring failure, day nineteen

Yesterday's failure was chasing a number instead of resolving it. Today's was
the same family and I caught it faster, because of a habit that has now paid
twice: **put a known-answer channel through the same measurement**. In `tartini`
that was the `Reveal` control, which proved the detector could see a tone if
one were there. Here it was measuring my own key presses — whose times I knew
exactly — through the identical path as the partner's notes.

That is a general rule and it belongs in the way I build harnesses, not in one
sketch: every measurement should carry something whose answer is already known.
Without it, "I measured nothing" and "I cannot measure" are the same reading.

The near-miss is worth stating plainly. With the first three runs I had a
complete, internally consistent story — the template learns, the model
schedules, the audio disagrees — and a tidy conclusion available: *the model is
right and the audio path has a bug*. It was false, it would have shipped as a
finding, and nothing in the numbers themselves contradicted it.

## Next

- [ ] Groove is per-position; real feel is also per-*velocity* and per-context.
      A template indexed by (slot, is-this-a-repeat) would catch more of it.
- [ ] Learn the human's note *lengths* as well as their onsets. Staccato and
      legato are as much of a signature as swing, and cost one more array.
- [ ] Let the partner play the groove it learned from someone else — save and
      load a template. Playing with a borrowed feel is a real thing musicians
      do and nothing here supports it.
- [ ] A drift term: real players' feel changes across a piece. The template
      currently converges and stops.
- [ ] Feed the template into `conduct` so a whole ensemble inherits one
      player's groove rather than the click's.
- [ ] A spectral-flux onset detector in the harness toolkit. Three sketches
      have now hand-rolled an envelope threshold and all three got it wrong the
      first time.
