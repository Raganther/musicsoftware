# 2026-09-19 — afteryou: free improvisation is a network protocol, and it has the protocol's bug

**Sketches touched:** `sketches/afteryou`
**Settings worth keeping:** the defaults — 4 players, 12 m, patience 2 s, phrase
1.2 s, reaction 0.18 s, seed 7, D dorian from root 47. Two demonstrations, both
one control: walk `Room` from 0.5 to 40 m and the clash rate goes 0.35% → 24.95%
with nobody playing any differently; set `Players` to 2 and `After a clash` to
`polite` and they never get out of each other's way again.

## What I tried

Improvisation and interaction, the stalest family — last visited 2026-09-14 with
`drag`. The unstruck ideas there are nearly all consensus-dynamics follow-ons to
`drag` and `entrain`, which would have been a third sketch about *tempo*. This is
about *who plays*, which nothing here has touched.

The observation: improvisers with no pulse between them take turns the only way
anybody can — wait a while, listen, and if nobody is playing, play. That is
**carrier sense multiple access**, the rule a shared radio channel uses, and it
fails the same way. Sound crosses a room at 343 m/s, so two players who start
within one travel time of each other *cannot have heard each other*. Both are
committed before either finds out. The vulnerable window is the room, and
listening harder does not narrow it.

`drag` found that delay makes an ensemble play slower than any of them intends.
This is the same delay doing something else entirely, to a quantity `drag` never
looks at.

## What I measured

Everything is `sketches/afteryou/floor.ts`, the module the sketch plays, checked
in node before anything made a sound.

**The known-answer channel first.** Switch listening off and every player is an
alternating renewal process — Exp(patience) idle, a fixed phrase busy — so for
g = phrase/patience there are exact finite-N expressions to hit:

| | worst departure | cells |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P(turn not overlapped) = [e^−g/(1+g)]^(N−1) | **4.08%** | 10 of 12 |
| time exactly one plays = N g/(1+g)^N | **1.08%** | 12 of 12 |

The two cells left out of the first are the ones where the prediction is 0.26%
and 0.0024% — 317 and 11 clean turns out of a hundred thousand. A ratio on
eleven events is not a departure, and reporting 39.81% as the worst departure,
which is what the first pass did, would have been reporting arithmetic on noise.

That law is the finite-N form of pure ALOHA: holding G = Ng fixed and growing N
gives Abramson's e^−2G, with throughput G·e^−2G peaking at 1/(2e) = 0.18394.
The closed form against that limit at G = 0.25 — arithmetic, not simulation —
is 1.1395 / 1.0336 / **1.0083** for N of 4 / 16 / 64.

**The room is the entire mechanism.** Set every travel time to zero, leave
everything else alone, and over **72,927 turns in six configurations** of player
count and manner the collision count is **exactly 0**. There is no other source
of clashes in this model; if a clash happens, a metre caused it.

**The law, and the correction it needed.** A player can only start when the floor
sounds clear, so the density of starts inside the vulnerable window is starts per
second *of silence*, not starts per second. With that, P(clean) = exp(−2·r·Στ):

| room | mean τ | naive ratio | predicted | measured | ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 m | 1.17 ms | 0.9976 | 99.65% | 99.65% | 1.0000 |
| 2 m | 4.69 ms | 0.9910 | 98.60% | 98.68% | 1.0008 |
| 6 m | 14.08 ms | 0.9715 | 95.86% | 95.92% | 1.0006 |
| 12 m | 28.15 ms | 0.9424 | 91.81% | 91.79% | 0.9998 |
| 24 m | 56.31 ms | 0.8890 | 84.00% | 84.08% | 1.0010 |
| 40 m | 93.85 ms | 0.8284 | 74.75% | 75.05% | 1.0040 |

**Worst departure 0.40%** over an 80× range of room size, and 0.42% over five
patience settings from 0.8 s to 12 s. The `naive ratio` column is the same
prediction using the raw start rate: fine in a small room, **17% out at forty
metres**. The whole correction is one division by the silence fraction, and it is
the difference between a law and an excuse.

**Waiting for the gap is what makes you collide.** `pouncing` — sit out a busy
floor and enter the moment it clears — runs **1.33–3.12×** over that law across
the same room sizes, because everybody's entry is now timed off the same event
rather than off their own clock. In absolute terms, at half a metre it collides
**9.4× more often** than `patient` in the identical room. Patience is not a
personality, it is a decorrelator.

**The best result is the deadlock.** After a clash both players break off, both
wait, both go again. Write it out: if i started at t_i and j at t_j, then i stops
at t_j + τ + r_i and j at t_i + τ + r_j, i hears j stop at t_i + 2τ + r_j and
retries r_i later, j likewise — and the difference between the two retry times is
**t_i − t_j exactly**, for any response they both make and whatever their
separate reaction times. The offset is conserved. So if it was inside the
vulnerable window it still is, forever.

| after a clash | turns | collision rate | longest consecutive run | time exactly one plays |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| none | 17,297 | 99.97% | 17,292 | 13.64% |
| polite | 10,479 | 99.95% | 10,474 | 8.32% |
| random | 1,871 | 3.85% | 6 | 54.05% |

And the offsets, printed to nine places, lock onto −0.027425399 s at turn 7 and
never move again. Everybody taking the same considerate pause is not politeness;
the only thing that resolves a clash is disagreeing about how long to wait.

**A third player does what politeness cannot.** Two players on `polite` clash
99.95% of the time. Three clash **19.98%**, four 17.52%, six 19.61%. The crowd
supplies the randomness the rule refuses to, because a third entry lands
wherever it likes and breaks the symmetry. A duo is the worst case, which is not
what I expected and is the opposite of how the loudness version behaves in
`lombard`.

**From the sound.** The floor is a schedule, so the audio is checked against it
rather than by detecting onsets. Frames the model says nobody is in must be
quiet and frames it says somebody is in must not be:

| | busy rms | clear rms | separation | frames |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4 players, 12 m | 0.13897 | 0.001013 | **137×** | 3743 / 153 |
| 2 players, 40 m | 0.13668 | 0.004581 | **30×** | 2708 / 832 |
| 6 players, 2 m | 0.13984 | — | no clear frame at all | 4048 / 0 |

The third row is not a failure, it is a result: with six players in a two-metre
room there is not one moment in 24 seconds with three quarters of a second of
clear air in it. The room has no silence left to take a turn in.

And frame by frame, every stretch the model calls clear is under the threshold
in the recording — **152 of 152, 122 of 122, 462 of 464** across the room sweep
— while heard-busy tracks model-busy at a constant 0.935–0.940, the ratio being
the decay tails the threshold cuts off.

**The deadlock is audible, and it is the predicted length.** If the offset is
conserved the two lock into a cycle of 2τ + r₀ + r₁ + the pause, which the
envelope's autocorrelation can be asked for without consulting the schedule:

| after a clash | predicted cycle | strongest lag | strength | clash rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| polite | 0.8502 s | **0.8500 s** | 0.803 | 100.0% |
| none | 0.5502 s | **0.5500 s** | 0.765 | 100.0% |
| random | 0.5502 s | 0.5550 s | **0.133** | 40.0% |

Both locked cases land within **one 5 ms frame** of the prediction, which is the
resolution. The runner-up peak is nearly as strong (0.787, 0.763) and that is
expected rather than worrying — a periodic envelope peaks at every multiple of
its period. The control that does the work is `random`, where the same
measurement on the same two players finds 0.133.

Levels: **0.345 at the defaults** and **0.861** at the loudest reachable
setting, over eleven configurations covering every corner of the panel, with
the transient on each change measured separately. Nothing scheduled behind the
playhead in any of them. The defaults sit at the quiet end of the house band and
that is the price of the paragraph below.

## What went wrong

**One shared random stream coupled the players, and the known-answer channel
caught it.** The deaf-mode law was 1.4–3.1% out in the cells with tens of
thousands of turns behind them — small, ordered, and far too consistent to be
sampling. The closed form assumes the players are independent; a single `rng`
serving every draw in the simulation makes how many numbers player A consumes
depend on what player B is doing. One stream per player, plus a separate one for
the notes, took the same cells from 1.0138 / 0.9688 / 0.9496 to 1.0002 / 1.0006 /
1.0009. The separate notes stream is worth as much: without it, changing a tune
moves every start time in the piece.

**I tried to tighten a loose test and made it circular.** `2τ/W` should predict
how often a randomised backoff clashes again, and the obvious sample — every turn
whose player had been clashed exactly once — gave 4.82 / 9.67 / 19.71 / 29.49%
against 4.37 / 8.75 / 17.49 / 29.15%: right, but consistently ~10% high. I
guessed the sample was polluted by retries that found the floor busy and came
back later, and selected instead on both players retrying within one backoff
window of the clash. That went to 14.08% against 4.37% — three times worse.

The selection was the problem. **Requiring two retries to land within one window
of the same instant is most of the way to requiring them to collide**, so I had
selected for the outcome I was predicting.

The right split is on one player at a time, and it explains the residual
completely (two players throughout, so `background` is the two-player rate):

| room | 2τ/W | all | prompt retries | deferred | background |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 m | 4.37% | 5.03% | 8.46% | 1.42% | 1.87% |
| 12 m | 8.75% | 8.75% | 11.88% | 5.23% | 3.33% |
| 24 m | 17.49% | 19.69% | 26.37% | 10.74% | 7.33% |
| 40 m | 29.15% | 29.01% | 36.59% | 17.39% | 12.86% |

Deferred retries do sit at the background rate, as guessed. But prompt ones run
**1.26–1.94×** above 2τ/W, because a retry only happens *promptly* if you were
the one who got in first — and the player who is first can only be talked over
from one side, which halves the window and doubles the conditional rate. The two
selections pull opposite ways and cancel. **The pooled agreement was luck**, and
the number I would have published is right for the wrong reason.

**The level took five passes and four of them were the wrong idea.** It started
at 0.111, which is inaudible next to everything else here, and the worst setting
clipped at 1.199. Then, in order:

1. Normalise by the number of *players* sounding at once. Still clipped — what
   sums is notes, and a 4 s phrase rings four at once where a 1.2 s one rings
   two.
2. Normalise by the number of *notes*. Still clipped, and worse: the same
   setting measured **1.199, 1.700 and 1.178 on three runs**. When a level
   measurement is not reproducible the level is not the problem.
3. The reproducibility was the clue. Each player's notes decayed 0.55 s over a
   0.24 s spacing, so one player rang four notes at once — a player playing
   chords with themselves, which is not the premise. **A player is one
   instrument.** Ending each note at the next bounded what can sound by the
   player count, and the spread collapsed.
4. That left 0.993, pinned across two different gains, which is a ceiling and not
   a measurement. It was the transient from flipping `Nobody listens` while six
   players held four-second phrases: a gain computed per note *at schedule time*
   cannot answer a surge, because the notes are already booked.

The fix is a gain node rather than a per-note number, and the lookahead makes it
predictive — the floor runs 0.8 s ahead of the sound, so the window that sets it
is mostly still in the future and it starts moving before the surge is audible.
That took the worst case from 0.993 to 0.861 and the pathological setting from
0.993 to 0.302. The exponent is 0.75 rather than the 0.5 an incoherent sum wants,
because a peak is not an RMS.

Two things worth keeping from it. The harness has to tap **after** the
normaliser, or it measures the stage before the one under test — the same error
`contrary` made with its WaveShaper. And the peak of a sparse random texture has
a genuine ±50% spread between 15-second windows, so a level cannot be tuned to a
tight band by one reading; the gate that matters is the ceiling, not the target.

**And I reloaded the page under my own harness.** Editing a sketch file while a
Playwright run was mid-capture triggered Vite's HMR, `window.__afteryou` went
away, and the capture came back empty. It failed loudly because the harness
checks, which is the only reason it cost ten minutes instead of a wrong table.

## Next

- [ ] Anticipation, which `drag` also wants: start toward where you predict the
      floor will be rather than where you hear it. It cannot close this window —
      the information has not arrived — so the interesting question is what it
      trades for what, and this is the sketch that can answer it.
- [ ] Non-persistent versus 1-persistent is two points; real improvisers are
      p-persistent, entering a gap with some probability. That sweep should show
      a minimum, and where it sits is a claim about ensemble size.
- [ ] Asymmetric earshot. `drag` found the tempo belongs to whoever is attended
      to; here the analogue is a player nobody can hear, who should be able to
      play continuously without ever being asked to stop.
- [ ] Collision detection is currently perfect — everybody breaks off. A player
      who *does not yield* is the whole of how some ensembles actually work and
      is one branch in `begin`.
- [ ] The deadlock needs a listener. It is a conserved quantity and a very clear
      graph, but nobody has said whether two players locked in a 0.76 s clash
      cycle reads as a mistake or as a groove.
- [ ] Exponential backoff doubles the window per clash, which is the Ethernet
      rule and is almost certainly not the musical one. A window that grows with
      how long you have been waiting instead of how often you have failed is a
      different and more human policy.
