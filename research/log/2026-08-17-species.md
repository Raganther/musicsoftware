# 2026-08-17 — species: composing by elimination

**Sketches touched:** `sketches/species`
**Settings worth keeping:** seed 21, 11 notes, all five rules on — cantus
D-G-F#-B-C#-B-A-G-F#-E-D, 2,115 legal counterpoints, the largest space I found
at the defaults. Seed 12 (1,501) and seed 44 (1,475) are close behind; seed 31
gives 409 and settles much faster, which is the better one to demonstrate the
collapse on. The thing to actually do: start the transport, listen to four or
five passes without touching anything, then commit the one note you keep
wanting to hear and listen again.

## What I tried

Composition tools, the stalest family (last visited 2026-08-11, tonnetz).

Every generative sketch in this repo hands you one answer and lets you press
the button again. I wanted the inverse: a tool that holds *every* answer, tells
you how many there are, and lets you compose by removing rather than by
choosing. First-species counterpoint is the right test case because the rules
are finite, old, and famously feel like they have exactly one solution.

So: a cantus firmus, a set of candidate pitches for the counterpoint above it,
and a dynamic program over (previous note, note before that). That state is
exactly wide enough for the whole rule set — consonance looks at one position,
parallels and melodic intervals at two, leap recovery and the ban on three
identical intervals at three. It gives exact counts, exact per-note marginals
and uniform sampling in about a millisecond, so the number on screen is the
number and the brightness of every square is a probability.

The sound is the point, not a garnish. Each pass through the cantus is a fresh
uniform draw, so with nothing committed the upper line shimmers — same
harmony, different tune every time. Commit a note and it stops moving there.
Commit enough and the shimmer stops entirely. You are listening to a piece
being decided.

## What I measured

The claim is not "here is a nice counterpoint" but "there are exactly N of them
and this is a fair draw", and both halves are checkable. The oracle is a brute
force enumerator written from the rules, with no dynamic programming near it,
living in the harness rather than the sketch.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| counts, 3 cantus firmi × 7 rule subsets | **21 of 21 exact** |
| marginals vs enumerated proportions, 56 squares | largest disagreement **0.00e+0** |
| single-note commitments vs brute force, 56 of them | **0 disagreed** |
| squares drawn as available that lead nowhere | **0** |

Fairness, on a 30-line space, 60,000 draws, expected 2,000 each: observed
**1,913–2,070**, chi-square **21.9 on 29 df** (uniform exceeds ~55 once in a
thousand). On the real 2,115-line problem, 10,000 draws produced 2,097 distinct
lines and **not one rule break**.

The collapse, seed 21, committing the most certain note each time:

    2115 → 2115 → 2115 → 1390 → 834 → 429 → 198 → 90 → 36 → 12 → 4 → 1

The first two commitments change nothing, because the opening and the close
were never free. Eleven notes, nine decisions. And the headline number is
smaller than I expected — a few hundred to a couple of thousand, not millions.
Fux exercises feel like there is one right answer; there are about two
thousand, which is a much more interesting fact than either extreme.

Which rule does the work, on one 11-note cantus:

| | counterpoints |
| --- | --- |
| all five rules | 144 |
| without *no 3 alike in a row* | 201 |
| without *no parallel 5ths/8ves* | 371 |
| without *no hidden 5ths/8ves* | 441 |
| without *formal open and close* | 1,750 |
| without *singable line* | **10,885** |

The melodic rule is worth more than the other four together. Two-part harmony
is barely a constraint; what makes counterpoint hard is being a tune.

Levels: pre-limiter peak 0.505 measured, 0.423 in the suite. Green: 24
sketches + jam, no clipping, clean teardown.

## Two rules I wrote wrong, and one measurement I could not make

The enumerator earned its keep immediately: with all rules on it returned **0**
for every cantus I tested, which meant the sketch was unusable at its own
defaults and I had not noticed because a shimmering wall of nothing looks
plausible for a second.

Both faults were in the cadence. I had written the textbook rule — a major
sixth before the final octave — and the candidate set started at the octave
above the root, so the leading tone the rule requires was not in it. Nothing
could ever satisfy it. Worse, the rule is wrong for most of the modes on offer:
a major sixth before the close assumes a leading tone that Dorian and Aeolian
do not have. Restating it as what it actually is — the cantus steps down to the
final, the counterpoint steps up into the octave against it — fixed both, and
is shorter.

The second was leap recovery at a fourth instead of Fux's fifth. Defensible as
style, but it costs a factor of about seven in the size of the space, and the
lines it removes are unobjectionable.

What I could **not** measure is the audio path. A detector that pulls the two
voices out of the stereo field by their pan positions and reads their pitches
by harmonic sum agrees with what the sketch says it is playing on 92.0% of
readings for the counterpoint and 82.9% for the cantus. That is not accurate
enough to audit counterpoint rules by ear — the "voice crossings" and
"dissonances" it reports are its own errors, since the solver is proven exact.
Two sustained voices a third apart share too many partials for this to work,
and the honest conclusion is that the rule audit has to happen on the lines the
sketch plays rather than on the loudspeaker. Those were checked: every one
legal, in both the free and the committed condition.

## The recurring failure, day sixteen

Different shape today, and a better one. Nothing was invented — the numbers all
came from an oracle, and the oracle disagreed with the solver three separate
times. Twice the solver was wrong (both cadence faults). Once the *oracle* was
wrong: I changed the leap threshold in the sketch and left the harness's copy
at the old value, and it reported 7,310 rule breaks out of 10,000 draws and a
chi-square of 38,224. A silent harness would have shipped that mismatch as a
finding in the other direction.

That is the argument for writing the oracle out longhand instead of importing
the solver's own predicate: it can disagree, and a disagreement is information
regardless of which side is wrong. Yesterday's rule was "the harness must not
import the sketch's idea of the answer". Today's addition is that the
duplication has a maintenance cost that shows up as a loud, obviously-wrong
number rather than a quiet one, which is the trade worth making.

The near-miss worth recording: I very nearly reported the audio detector's 5
"dissonances" as a finding about the sketch. They are detector errors. What
stopped it was that the solver had already been proven exact against brute
force, so a dissonance in the audio would have implied a bug in the *playback*
path, which is eight lines long and could not produce a wrong pitch without
producing a wrong pitch every time. The general form: when two measurements
disagree, work out which one has more room to be wrong before believing either.

## Next

- [ ] Second species — two notes against one — which needs passing tones and
      turns the state into (note, beat). The DP handles it; the rules triple.
- [ ] Let the user draw the cantus instead of taking what the seed gives.
      Everything downstream already re-derives; it is a UI job, not a solver
      one.
- [ ] Weight the draw by something other than uniform: prefer contrary motion,
      or a target contour. The DP already carries the weights, so this is a
      change of one multiplication.
- [ ] Show *why* a square is dark. Right now a dead cell is a dot; it could say
      which rule kills it, which would make the thing teach.
- [ ] Three voices. The state grows as the product, so this is where the
      dynamic program stops being free and starts needing thought.
- [ ] A "how surprising is this line" readout — the probability of the drawn
      line under the space is already computable and would let you keep the
      unlikely ones deliberately.
