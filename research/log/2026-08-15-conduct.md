# 2026-08-15 — conduct: they drag because they are listening

**Sketches touched:** `sketches/conduct`
**Settings worth keeping:** the defaults with seed 6 — 7 players, 18 ms
reaction, 10 ms looseness, follow 0.35 — is a small string section that arrives
a hair late and never quite together. The demo is dragging `Listen to each
other` from 0 to 0.85 while the transport runs: the dots on screen walk to the
right and the ensemble falls behind the click, with nobody's reaction time
having changed. Looseness 0 with follow 0 is the control: a machine.

## What I tried

Improvisation/performance, the stalest family (last visited 2026-08-09; the
14th's run did not fire, so this is the first since). An instrument you conduct
rather than play. Give a beat — click the strip, or let the transport give it —
and a small ensemble plays on it, each player with their own reaction time,
their own unsteadiness and their own tuning.

The thing I actually wanted was the second-order effect. Real players do not
only watch the stick; they correct toward what they *hear*, and what they hear
is already late. The correction chases a target that is itself behind the beat,
so the offsets satisfy `off = base + follow · off` and settle at
**base / (1 − follow)**. An ensemble drags not because anyone is slow but
because everyone is attentive, and the amount is predictable.

## What I measured

The click track sits at 5 kHz on purpose and the players below 1.5 kHz, so the
delay between stick and ensemble can be read off the audio rather than taken on
trust. Envelopes captured at ~3 kHz — a frame rate would have been far too
coarse for an effect measured in tens of milliseconds.

With every player sharing a 20 ms reaction time, so the prediction is exact:

| listen to each other | measured lag | base/(1 − follow) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.00 | 24.8 ± 2.3 ms | 20.0 |
| 0.25 | 31.3 ± 1.8 ms | 26.7 |
| 0.50 | 44.5 ± 1.8 ms | 40.0 |
| 0.75 | **80.2 ± 11.0 ms** | **80.0** |

Four for four. There is a constant ≈5 ms offset that belongs to the detector
rather than the model — the click rises in 1 ms and the ensemble in 8, so their
threshold crossings are not the same instant — and subtracting it gives 20.1,
26.6, 39.8, 75.5 against 20, 26.7, 40, 80.

Looseness widens the entry, measured beat to beat: 2.1, 4.0, 8.1, 11.2 ms of
wobble for looseness 0, 8, 20, 36 ms.

Peak 0.492 in the suite. Green: 22 sketches + jam, no clipping, clean teardown.

## The caveat that matters

That second table is **not** what it looks like at first glance. It is the
beat-to-beat variability of when the ensemble comes in, not the spread across
players *within* one beat. Every player is in the same frequency band, so a
spectrogram only ever sees the composite, and the composite's onset is set by
whoever arrives first. The within-beat spread — the thing the dots on screen
show, and the thing that actually makes an ensemble sound like more than one
person — is model state, not measurement, and the notes now say so.

I nearly wrote it up as the spread. It reads better that way and the numbers
are the same numbers. What stopped it was asking what the detector could
physically have seen, which is a question I should be asking before designing
the measurement rather than while writing it up.

## The recurring failure, day fourteen

Clean on the invention front: `TODO:measure` went in before the prose again,
and both tables came out of harness output. Two weeks of this now, and the
honest summary is that the marker works and the *habit* still has not formed —
I write the marker deliberately, every time, the way you'd use a checklist
rather than the way you'd use a reflex.

Today's near-miss was one layer out from that: not an invented number but an
invented *interpretation* of a real number. The measurement was sound, the
figure was sound, and the sentence describing it would have been false. A
`TODO:measure` marker does nothing about that, because the number was measured.
The question that catches it is different — *what could this detector actually
have distinguished?* — and it belongs in the design of the harness, not in the
writing.

Also worth recording as a plain process note: a template literal broke the

## Next

- [ ] Give the players separate spectral homes — even a couple of octaves apart
      — so the within-beat spread is measurable and not just drawn.
- [ ] Conduct with a gesture rather than a click: the beat should come from the
      *turnaround* of a drag, which is what a stick actually gives, and would
      let the ensemble anticipate rather than only react.
- [ ] A player who watches another player rather than the mean — a section
      leader — which is how real orchestras keep the drag bounded.
- [ ] Let `follow` exceed 1 and see what happens. The model says the lag
      diverges; an ensemble that cannot find the beat at all might be a more
      interesting sound than a well-behaved one.
- [ ] Tempo changes: the whole point of a conductor is *changing* the beat, and
      right now the ensemble only ever tracks a steady one.
