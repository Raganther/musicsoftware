# 2026-10-01 — stretch: the octave has four right answers and they are all wide

**Sketches touched:** `sketches/stretch`
**Settings worth keeping:** the defaults — B = 0.001, `4:2`, strike 1/8, 16
partials, root 45 pentatonic major, decay 2.6, room 0.26, seed 9. Then sweep
`Octave tuned for` at B = 0.004 without touching anything else: the pitch of the
upper string moves by 133 cents and every one of the four positions is correctly
tuned. `Strike point` 0.5 removes every even partial at once, which is a hollow,
clarinet-shaped pluck; 0.02 is the hard, bright end. B = 0.012 at root 33 is
where the low partials stop being a chord and start being a cluster.

## What I tried

Synthesis/DSP, the stalest family — last visited 2026-09-25 with `blare`. The
idea was sitting unstruck in `research/ideas.md`:

> The same rig with a *string* rather than a beam: the piano hammer problem,
> where the strike point kills the 8th partial and everyone can hear it.

The strike comb is the small half of that. Every string in this repo —
`aeolian-harp`, `bow`, `wolf` — is **ideal**: its partials are exact multiples
of the fundamental, because an ideal string restores only through tension. A
real wire also resists *bending*, which stiffens the short wavelengths, so
partial `n` sits at `n·f₀·√(1 + B·n²)`. Nothing here had that, and it is the
reason a piano cannot be tuned exactly rather than a refinement of its timbre.

Tuning an octave means making a partial of the lower note beat-free against a
partial of the upper. Which pair you choose is a *decision*, and with stretched
partials the decisions disagree. That disagreement is the sketch.

## What I measured

### The model, in node before anything sounded

`sketches/stretch/stiff.ts`. **Partials, in cents above where an ideal string
would have put them:**

| B | n=2 | n=4 | n=8 | n=16 | n=24 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0001 | 0.35 | 1.38 | 5.52 | 21.88 | 48.48 |
| 0.0010 | 3.46 | 13.74 | 53.70 | 197.30 | 393.76 |
| 0.0080 | 27.27 | 104.26 | 357.87 | 964.72 | 1492.49 |

At B = 0.001 — ordinary, middle-of-the-keyboard stiffness — the sixteenth
partial is **197 cents** sharp of its harmonic. Two semitones.

**So the octave has four right answers.** Width in cents of a beat-free `2k:k`
octave, both strings at the same B:

| B | 2:1 | 4:2 | 6:3 | 8:4 | worst disagreement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1200.00 | 1200.00 | 1200.00 | 1200.00 | **0.00** |
| 0.0002 | 1200.52 | 1202.07 | 1204.65 | 1208.24 | 7.72 |
| 0.0010 | 1202.59 | 1210.28 | 1222.86 | 1239.96 | **37.37** |
| 0.0040 | 1210.28 | 1239.96 | 1285.84 | 1343.60 | 133.32 |

At B = 0 every type agrees *exactly* — the disagreement is the stiffness and
nothing else. That row is the negative control, and it is exact rather than
small.

**And it has a closed form, which says more than the table.** For equal B the
width collapses to

    width(B, k) = 1200·log₂( 2·√(4 − 3/(1 + B·k²)) )

agreeing with `octaveCents` to **1.14e−12 cents** over 8 values of B × 200
values of k. Three things fall straight out of it that the table only hints at:

- `4 − 3/(1+Bk²) > 1` for any B > 0, so **every octave type is always wide.**
  Never narrow, for any stiffness, at any k. The smallest excess over 1200 I
  could find for B ∈ (1e−9, 1e−4] is 2.6e−6 cents — small, but positive.
- it increases in k, so **2:1 < 4:2 < 6:3 < 8:4 always**. The ordering in the
  table is a theorem, not an accident of the numbers I picked.
- it has a **ceiling of exactly 2400 cents**, because `4 − 3/(1+Bk²) → 4`. The
  widest conceivable "octave" between partial 2k and partial k approaches *two*
  octaves, however stiff the wire. At B = 0.001 the k = 16 pair already wants
  1613 cents.

**In beats you could count**, A2 to A3 at B = 0.001:

| octave width | beat at 2:1 | beat at 4:2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1200.00 | 0.330 Hz | 2.627 Hz |
| 1202.59 | **0.000** | 1.967 Hz |
| 1210.28 | 0.982 Hz | **0.000** |

You can null one or the other, not both, and the equal-tempered octave nulls
neither. `Octave tuned for` is exactly that choice.

**From wire geometry, with the tension derived rather than invented.** The first
version of this had me picking a tension per octave, and the answer moved by
3 cents when I picked differently — which means the number was mine and not the
wire's. The tension is not free: a string of length `L`, diameter `d`, density
`ρ`, tuned to `f₀`, is under `T = 4f₀²L²·ρπd²/4`, and substituting that into
`B = π³Ed⁴/(64L²T)` leaves

    B = π²·E·d² / (64·ρ·f₀²·L⁴)

which needs only the speaking length, the wire gauge and the pitch. On plain
steel wire from A2 up (below that the strings are wound, which adds mass
without stiffness and this formula stops applying):

| note | L (mm) | d (mm) | f₀ | B | T (N) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A2 | 750 | 1.05 | 110 | 1.13e−3 | 185 |
| A3 | 520 | 0.95 | 220 | 1.00e−3 | 291 |
| A4 | 345 | 0.90 | 440 | 1.16e−3 | 460 |
| A5 | 205 | 0.85 | 880 | 2.08e−3 | 580 |
| A6 | 115 | 0.80 | 1760 | 4.64e−3 | 647 |
| A7 | 55 | 0.75 | 3520 | 1.95e−2 | 520 |

The derived tensions land at 185–650 N, which is where real plain-wire piano
tensions sit — the geometry is not nonsense. B is **lowest in the middle** and
climbs steeply at the top, because `L` shortens faster than `1/f₀` once the
scale runs out of room. That shape is structural, not fitted. Chaining the five
octaves:

| chained type | top note | against equal temperament |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2:1 | 3540.54 Hz | **+10.08 cents** |
| 4:2 | 3602.21 Hz | +39.97 cents |
| 6:3 | 3704.96 Hz | +88.66 cents |
| 8:4 | 3848.51 Hz | +154.47 cents |

Always sharp — that part is the theorem above and does not depend on my
millimetres. *How* sharp depends on both the scale and the type, and the type
matters far more: swapping 2:1 for 4:2 moves the top note four times as far as
any plausible change to the lengths does. This is the Railsback stretch arriving
out of nothing but wire diameters, string lengths and the pitches they are tuned
to, and the reason tuners argue is visible in the second column.

### Off the sound

`scratchpad/xaudio2.mjs`, tapping the master **pre-limiter** through a worklet,
44.1 kHz. The partial frequencies are what the synth is built from, so measuring
those only checks the oscillators. Two things in the output are not put in
anywhere, and those are the measurements.

**The partial amplitudes.** The comb `|sin(nπx)|`, times `1/n`, times a decay of
`6/n^0.7` seconds — predicted as the Hann-weighted average of that envelope over
the same window the projection uses, so the prediction is of the measurement and
not of the peak:

| n | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 11 | 12 | 14 | 16 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| measured | 1.0000 | 0.8643 | 0.6435 | 0.3005 | 0.1207 | 0.0758 | 0.0075 | 0.0345 |
| predicted | 1.0000 | 0.8643 | 0.6433 | 0.3004 | 0.1206 | 0.0757 | 0.0075 | 0.0345 |

**1.000 on all sixteen**, worst error 0.1% with one free parameter (the onset,
fitted 4.4 ms off the detector) and 3.3% without it. Partial 14 is 0.75% of the
peak and still lands.

**The strike nulls.** Striking at `m/n` puts a node of partial `n` under the
hammer:

| struck at | partials removed | relative amplitude | vs its neighbours |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1/8 | 8 | 5.9e−9 | **−137.6 dB** |
| 1/8 | 16 | 6.9e−9 | −123.4 dB |
| 1/5 | 5, 10, 15, 20 | 1.0e−8 … 6.4e−8 | −115.3 … −124.2 dB |
| 0.07 | none | — | −10.5 dB at the deepest dip |

The control is the last row: at a strike point with no rational null under 24,
the deepest any partial gets below its neighbours is 10.5 dB, against 115+ for a
real null. This one is partly true by construction — `pluck` skips a partial
whose comb amplitude is zero, so what the audio proves is that nothing else in
the chain fills the hole, and that the floor of the measurement is −137 dB and
not −40.

**The beat rate**, which is the real measurement: it is the difference between
two partials of two different strings, it appears nowhere in the code, and it
shows up as an amplitude modulation of the mix. One note struck alone at 185 Hz,
B = 0.002, read off a narrowband envelope at the midpoint of the pair:

| tuned for | width | pair | predicted | measured | cycles | depth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| equal | 1200.00 | 2:1 | 1.107 Hz | **1.100** | 3.7 | 0.318 |
| | | 4:2 | 8.793 Hz | **8.790** | 17.1 | 0.278 |
| 2:1 | 1205.17 | 2:1 | 0.000 Hz | — | — | **0.065** |
| | | 4:2 | 6.571 Hz | **6.570** | 12.8 | 0.263 |
| 4:2 | 1220.37 | 2:1 | 3.276 Hz | **3.280** | 11.0 | 0.318 |
| | | 4:2 | 0.000 Hz | — | — | **0.039** |

Every live beat within 0.01 Hz of prediction — the resolution of the search
grid — and the two that were tuned out have a modulation depth of 0.065 and
0.039 against 0.26–0.32 for the live ones. The claim "you can null one or the
other but not both" is audible and counted, not asserted.

Levels: **0.426** at the defaults, **0.765** worst over ten settings. After
leaving the sketch mid-note the master fades with a time constant of **9.9 ms**
— the host asks for 10 — fitted by least squares on log amplitude and stable to
0.1 ms across the window chosen (9.91 / 9.89 / 9.81 over three). It reaches
exact zero after 113 ms and stays there: 2721 consecutive silent blocks, 7.9 s.
The first estimator I wrote for that time constant read 8.3 ms, because it
averaged block-to-block ratios and discarded any ratio above 1 — and the fade
starts while the partials are still beating, so the first few blocks are not
monotone and dropping them biases the mean down. The endpoints alone give 10.15.

## What went wrong

**A constant floor on an exponential ramp is a relative floor.** The note
envelope ended `exponentialRampToValueAtTime(Math.max(1e-5, gain·a·0.0008), …)`.
An exponential ramp cannot reach zero, so it needs a positive target — but a
*constant* one is a different number of dB down for a quiet partial than for a
loud one, and the quiet ones therefore stop decaying early and ring on as a
halo. Every partial from the twelfth up measured high, by 7%, 19%, **55%**, 37%,
20%, and the crossover sat exactly where `gain·a·0.0008` fell under 1e−5:
predicted 1.000 1.000 1.051 1.174 1.574 1.371 1.188 for n = 10…16 against
measured 1.022 1.024 1.072 1.186 1.548 1.365 1.202. Making the floor 1e−9, so
it never binds, took the worst error from 20.2% to 3.3%. This is a cousin of the
`cancelScheduledValues` trap already in `CLAUDE.md`: an AudioParam's absolute
numbers do not know how loud the thing they are shaping is.

**My cleanup defeated the host's anti-click fade.** `host.ts` fades a sketch's
bus out with a 10 ms time constant and disconnects at 120 ms, *precisely* so
that leaving mid-note does not click — but cleanup functions run **before** that
fade, so `bus.disconnect()` in cleanup cut the ringing partials instantly and
clicked anyway. Measured, the sound stopped dead inside one render quantum where
the fade would have left 13% of it 20 ms later. Handing the teardown to the same
timer (`setTimeout(…, 200)`) gives the fade back, and it is now visible in the
audio as a clean 10.1 ms exponential. Any sketch that disconnects its own graph
synchronously has this.

**`#/` is not an empty route.** I checked "nothing is audible after navigating
away" by navigating to `#/`, got a peak of 3.5e−2, and went looking for a leak
in `stretch`. There is none: `route()` does `id ? findSketch(id) : sketches[0]`,
so `#/` mounts the *first* sketch, which is `aeolian-harp`, which plays on its
own by design — its status line literally says "the wind plays while you don't".
The residual was at 329.8 / 440.1 / 661.7 Hz, nothing to do with the 185 Hz note
I had been playing, and it restarted every 2 s. An unknown id (`#/__nothing__`)
renders "Not found" and mounts nothing; that is the route that tests teardown,
and against it the reading is exactly 0.0 in all 2721 blocks after the fade. The
recipe in `CLAUDE.md` says to navigate away without saying where to, so I have
added it there.

**I analysed a note that was not sounding.** The first harness measured
amplitudes at `partial(mtof(45), B, n)` while the sketch was playing a *seeded
random walk* whose first note is whatever `r.int(-2, 3)` returned — in the event
midi 54, not 45. Every frequency in the analysis belonged to a note that was not
playing, and overlapping notes smeared the envelope on top of that. The fix is
two-part and worth keeping: strike exactly one note (start the transport, stop
it again before the second one — the note is already scheduled on the audio
graph and rings on), and recover its pitch *from the recording* by scanning
equal-tempered candidates and taking the lowest strong one, so the analysis can
never again assume a pitch the sketch did not play.

**And the row of the notes table I computed by hand was wrong.** 205.77 and
429.45 cents where the function says 197.30 and 393.76. A table in a `notes`
field looks like data and is read like data; mine was arithmetic I did in my
head. Everything quoted is now printed by a script.

## Next

- [ ] **Two strings per note, slightly apart, which is what a piano has.** Three
      unisons at the same pitch beat against each other and the tuner sets those
      too; the sketch currently has one string per note and so has no unison.
- [ ] **A real hammer instead of a comb.** The strike comb assumes a point
      contact at an instant. A felt hammer has a width (which low-passes the
      comb, so the nulls fill in and the high partials soften) and a contact
      time (which cannot excite a partial whose period is shorter than it).
      `mallet` already has Chaigne–Askenfelt contact; this is that against a
      string, and it would make the nulls *approximate*, which is the honest
      version of the claim this sketch makes exactly.
- [ ] **Stiffness as a tuning instrument rather than a piano simulation.** B is
      a continuous knob from harmonic to bell-like, and the scale that sounds
      consonant on a stiff string is not 12-TET. Sethares' dissonance curve over
      a stretched spectrum gives a *different* optimal scale per B — one slider
      that retunes the keyboard to match the timbre.
- [ ] **The 2400-cent ceiling is audible and untested.** At B = 0.012 the k = 8
      pair wants 1343 cents; the sketch only offers k up to 4. A `k` that goes
      to 16 would let you hear an "octave" of nearly a twelfth that is
      nevertheless beat-free, which is the strangest consequence of the closed
      form and currently only a number.
- [ ] Measure a real piano note and fit B to it, rather than choosing B. The
      formula says B is fixed by `d²/(f₀²L⁴)`; a recording would say whether the
      partials of an actual instrument follow `√(1 + Bn²)` as far as n = 16 or
      drift off it, and the drift is where soundboard coupling lives.
- [ ] The strike comb and the octave choice are independent in this sketch and
      are not in a piano: the hammer line is positioned to suppress the partials
      that would otherwise make the tuner's life impossible. Striking at 1/8
      kills partial 8 and so **removes the 8:4 octave as an option entirely**.
      That interaction is implemented and unexplored.
