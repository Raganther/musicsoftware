# 2026-08-11 — tonnetz: two voices hold, one moves

**Sketches touched:** `sketches/tonnetz`
**Settings worth keeping:** the defaults with seed 4 — P 0.5 / L 0.8 / R 0.9,
`Stay in key` 0.35 — is the reference walk: mostly diatonic, wandering out and
back over a minute or so. Set `Stay in key` to 0 and it leaves the key within
about four moves and never returns. The demo is switching `Mode` to `random`
mid-play and back: same voices, same voicing solver, completely different music.

## What I tried

Composition tools, the stalest family (last visited 2026-08-05). The Tonnetz:
a lattice where every triangle is a triad and adjacent triangles share two
notes. The three neo-Riemannian moves between neighbours —

- **P** (parallel): C major ↔ C minor, the third moves a semitone
- **L** (leading-tone): C major ↔ E minor, the root moves a semitone
- **R** (relative): C major ↔ A minor, the fifth moves a tone

— are the ones nineteenth-century composers used to get between distant keys
without anything sounding like a modulation. Three sustained voices walk the
lattice; two hold and one moves, every time.

I built the control into the sketch rather than the harness. `random` mode
picks any of the 24 triads instead of a neighbour, and runs it through the
*same* voicing solver — all six assignments of voices to chord tones, each
realised in the octave nearest its voice, lowest total motion wins. So any
difference between the modes is about which chord comes next and not about how
carefully it is voiced. It is also the fastest way to hear what the lattice
buys, which is why it stayed in as a param.

## What I measured

Sounding pitch classes read out of the spectrum, consecutive chords compared.
60 s per mode, around 48 chords each:

| | voice-leading distance | notes held in common |
| --- | --- | --- |
| parsimonious | **1.40** semitones — only ever 1 or 2 | **2.00 of 3, in 47 of 47** |
| random | 3.79 semitones — spread 1 to 6 | 0.59 of 3 |

The distance histogram in parsimonious mode is `{1: 28, 2: 19}` and contains
nothing else, which is exactly P and L costing one semitone and R costing two.
Weighted by the default P/L/R weights the predicted mean is 1.41; measured
1.40. And the common-tone count is not "usually two" — it was two in every one
of 47 transitions.

**The measurement checks itself.** 100% of the chords it read in parsimonious
mode are real triads. In random mode only 80% are: more voices move at once, so
more samples land mid-change and get read as a chord that was never played.
That makes the random column the weaker of the two, and I would not quote 3.79
to two decimal places for anything. The direction and the size of the gap are
not in doubt; the second decimal is.

Peak 0.445 over a 60 s run, 0.349 in the suite's shorter window. Green: 19
sketches + jam, no clipping, clean teardown.

## The recurring failure, day eleven — the one I dodged

Yesterday I wrote that the failures of the past fortnight were all in places
where the claim was about *feel*, and that the week had not tested that. Today
was the natural test: a composition tool, the most feel-laden family there is.

I dodged it. I picked, deliberately, the part of the idea that has a number
attached — voice-leading distance in semitones — and measured that beautifully.
What I did not touch is the actual premise of the sketch, which is that
parsimonious voice leading is *why* you can travel to a distant key without it
sounding like a modulation. That is a claim about perception. I cannot measure
it with a Playwright harness, and 1.40 semitones per move is not evidence for
it; it is evidence that the mechanism is doing what the mechanism should.

So the sketch's notes now say that explicitly: the perceptual claim is the
reason the sketch exists, not a finding. That is the best I can currently do,
and writing "prefer designs whose central claim comes with a number" three days
running is starting to look less like a discipline and more like a way of only
ever picking claims I can win. The honest next step is a sketch whose central
claim is perceptual, with the perceptual question put to a listener — which
means asking the user, not the harness.
