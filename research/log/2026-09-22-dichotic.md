# 2026-09-22 — dichotic: neither ear receives the music, and you hear it anyway

**Sketches touched:** `sketches/dichotic`
**Settings worth keeping:** the defaults — 48 notes, composed lines, crossing
rate 0.05, alternate scatter, seed 4, A major from root 57. The demonstration is
the `Listen` select, on headphones: `as sent` is the truth, `as grouped` is the
percept made physical, and `left only` is neither. At the defaults each ear
leaps 6.29 degrees a step and the streams move 0.48.

## What I tried

Genuinely strange, the stalest family — last visited 2026-09-17 with `contrary`.
Everything in the `Wild` section is an illusion *in the signal*: `tartini` and
`contrary` put melodies in difference tones, `veil` hides one under masking,
`hollow` cancels one to nothing. All of them measurable, because the fact is in
the wire.

Nothing here has been binaural at all. Diana Deutsch's scale illusion (1975)
sends an ascending scale to one ear and a descending one to the other,
alternating which ear gets which note. Nobody hears what was sent: each ear
receives a sequence leaping about, and listeners report a smooth high line on
one side and a smooth low line on the other. **Hearing groups by pitch
proximity before it groups by ear.**

That is central rather than physical, so there is nothing in the wire to point
at — the honest problem this sketch has and the others did not. What *is*
exactly computable is the combinatorics: regrouping two notes into two streams
is a two-state assignment, so "what a listener grouping by proximity would
hear" is a Viterbi with an exact optimum, and can be put beside what was
composed and what each ear actually received. That turns an illusion into a
composition tool with a number on it.

## What I measured

Everything is `sketches/dichotic/stream.ts`, checked in node first.

**The gate is the case with a known answer.** If the two lines never swap which
is higher, any proximity rule must recover them exactly, whatever the
scattering did:

| scatter | crossing-free pieces | by height | min motion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| alternate | 60 of 60 | **1.0000** | **1.0000** |
| random | 60 of 60 | **1.0000** | **1.0000** |
| none | 60 of 60 | **1.0000** | **1.0000** |

**The size of it.** Mean absolute step in scale degrees, over 60 pieces:

| scatter | per ear | per stream | composed lines | ear ÷ stream |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| none | 0.400 | 0.400 | 0.400 | 1.00× |
| random | 2.955 | 0.400 | 0.400 | 7.38× |
| alternate | **5.382** | **0.400** | 0.400 | **13.45×** |

The per-stream figure is *identical* to the composed lines' own, which is the
recovery restated — the scattering moves each ear 13.45× further from a smooth
line while leaving the music exactly where it was. Deutsch's own scale pair
gives 3.952 per ear against 1.000 per stream.

**And the classic pair is not recovered, which is the point.** The two scales
meet in the middle, so they *do* cross, and what a proximity listener gets is
the bouncing contour rather than either scale:

```
left ear   0 7 2 5 4 3 6 1 8 1 6 3 4 5 2 7
right ear  8 1 6 3 4 5 2 7 0 7 2 5 4 3 6 1
heard high 8 7 6 5 4 5 6 7 8 7 6 5 4 5 6 7
heard low  0 1 2 3 4 3 2 1 0 1 2 3 4 3 2 1
```

Recovery against the composed lines is 0.5625 and the illusion is total. It is
not that the listener reassembles the score; it is that the listener assembles
something nobody wrote.

**Where the two grouping rules disagree is a compositional handle.** By-height
grouping can never hear a crossing — it bounces at every one, **0.0% passed
through at every rate tested**, which is a definition rather than a result and
is the check that the crossing detector works. Minimum-motion grouping is
allowed to cross, and how often it does depends on how fast the lines converge:

| crossings per step | 0.05 | 0.10 | 0.20 | 0.40 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| crossings seen | 182 | 373 | 757 | 1506 |
| min motion passed through | **39.6%** | 32.4% | 16.2% | **7.7%** |

Lines that converge slowly linger near each other, so passing through costs
almost no motion. Lines that sweep through are already far apart on the other
side, so a stream that crossed would have to leap and bouncing is cheaper.
**How fast your lines approach decides whether a listener hears them cross or
bounce** — a thing to compose with, and not a fact about the notes.

I wrote the prediction down first: minimum-motion would bounce too, because
crossing costs more motion. It is wrong at slow crossings and right at fast
ones, and the crossover is somewhere near 0.1 crossings per step.

**The generator does what it says.** The crossing rate is a property of the gap
between the lines and nothing else, so it is written as a centre plus a cosine
gap whose sign changes are the crossings — measured against the predicted
`cross·n`:

| cross | predicted | measured | ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.05 | 3.2 | 3.03 | 0.948 |
| 0.10 | 6.4 | 6.22 | 0.971 |
| 0.20 | 12.8 | 12.62 | 0.986 |
| 0.40 | 25.6 | 25.10 | 0.980 |

**From the sound, one ear at a time.** Every previous harness here summed to
mono, because the capture worklet's `l` is channel 0 and that is the documented
trap. It posts `r` too, and this is the first sketch that needs it: the whole
claim is that the two channels carry *different* sequences, so the test is a
per-channel pitch attribution against the schedule. In each step window the left
channel should hold the pitch the model sent left and essentially none of the
one it sent right. Steps where the two lines land on the same degree are
skipped, because there the test is vacuous rather than passing.

| Listen | steps | wrong in left | wrong in right | separation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| as sent | 52 | **0** | **0** | **38.7×** |
| as grouped | 51 | **0** | **0** | 23.0× |
| mono | 53 | 29 | 24 | **1.0×** |

Nothing misattributed either way round, and `mono` is the negative control that
says the measurement could have failed: with both notes in both ears the
separation is exactly 1.0× and the attribution is at chance, 29 and 24 of 53.

And the sketch is playing the pieces the numbers came from — ear jaggedness to
four decimals and crossing counts, sketch against node model, 5 of 5 identical.

Levels: **0.475** at the defaults and 0.717 worst over six settings.

## What went wrong

**The first generator could not set the thing the whole experiment sweeps.** It
walked the two lines independently and pushed them together with a probability,
which produced 2.9 crossings per 64 steps *whatever* it was asked for — a
perfectly flat column across a ten-fold sweep, which I read past twice before
noticing the numbers were the same. A crossing is a sign change of the gap, so
the gap is what has to be written; as a centre and a cosine the rate comes out
right by construction and can be checked against a formula.

**And my crossing detector skipped the crossings.** It compared signs and
ignored zeros, so a pair of lines that *meet* on a degree and turn — which is
exactly what Deutsch's scales do — counted as never crossing. That is how 7
pieces of 60 got reported as crossing-free while failing a test that
crossing-free pieces cannot fail.

**Then the test for it was vacuous.** To ask whether a stream passed through a
crossing I compared the two notes at that step as a *set* against the two
composed notes — which always matches, because both streams always hold both
notes. It read 100.0% for both rules at every rate, a clean confirming table
measuring nothing. The question is about *identity over time*: which line each
stream was following before the crossing and after it.

Three failures in a row with the same shape — a quantity that looked measured
and was not — and all three were caught by the same habit: a column that does
not move when its input does, or a number that is the same for two methods that
must differ.

## Next

- [ ] Onset synchrony as the other grouping cue. The notes here start together
      by construction; stagger them and the streams should re-form by *time*
      instead of by pitch, which is the competing rule and would fight this one.
- [ ] The illusion needs the two ears to be roughly equal in loudness. Sweeping
      an imbalance should break it at a threshold, and where the threshold sits
      is the sort of number this sketch is built to report.
- [ ] A third stream. Deutsch's demo is two lines and two ears; three lines
      scattered over two ears is a harder assignment and nobody has asked what
      happens.
- [ ] Point `shorthand` at the ear sequences. Yesterday's parser finds the
      cheapest description of a line — the composed lines should compress and
      the scattered ears should not, which is the same claim in bits.
- [ ] Deutsch's octave illusion, which is the same machinery with one tone and
      two octaves, and produces a *different* percept in each ear. The scatter
      code already does everything except the octave.
- [ ] Whether anybody actually hears it is still unmeasured, exactly as `veil`
      left it. This sketch reports what a proximity listener would hear, not
      what a listener does — and that gap is honest but it is still a gap.
