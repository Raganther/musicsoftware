# 2026-09-06 — canon: what it costs to be your own second voice

**Sketches touched:** `sketches/canon`
**Settings worth keeping:** the defaults — length 14, delay 2, imitation −4 (a
fifth below), D major, seed 7. That seed's first draw is
`0 0 5 0 3 5 4 3 4 1 4 6 2 2`, which is the melody every audio number below was
measured on. Set imitation to −2 and the count nearly doubles; set the delay to
4 and it quadruples.

## What I tried

Composition tools, the stalest family — last visited 2026-09-01 with `pivot`.

`species` composes a first-species counterpoint by holding *every* legal answer
at once and letting you fix notes until the count reaches one. It is the best
tool in the repo and the idea generalises, so the question was what to point it
at next. A canon, because a canon is the same question asked of a much less
forgiving object: the second voice is not an independent line, it **is** the
first one, delayed.

That single change alters the shape of the problem. In a counterpoint a note has
one job. In a canon it has two — sung once as melody, and again d notes later as
harmony against whatever the melody has since become — so committing to a note
constrains the future *and* the past. The dynamic program has to widen to match:
the harmonic rule reads notes i and i−d, the parallels rule also reads i−1 and
i−d−1, so the state is the last d+1 notes rather than the last two. Eight
candidate degrees and a delay of four is 32,768 states, which is the ceiling and
a real one.

## What I measured

**It agrees with brute force, which is the only reason anything below counts.**
Eight configurations with the span, length, delay and interval of imitation all
varied, each small enough to enumerate every melody:

| span | n | d | imitation | brute force | DP | worst marginal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4 | 8 | 1 | 5th below | 176 | 176 | 0.0e+0 |
| 4 | 8 | 2 | 5th below | 235 | 235 | 0.0e+0 |
| 4 | 8 | 3 | 5th below | 691 | 691 | 0.0e+0 |
| 5 | 7 | 2 | unison | 807 | 807 | 0.0e+0 |
| 5 | 7 | 2 | 3rd below | 751 | 751 | 0.0e+0 |
| 4 | 9 | 4 | 5th below | 2,625 | 2,625 | 0.0e+0 |
| 5 | 8 | 2 | 5th below | 2,343 | 2,343 | 0.0e+0 |
| 4 | 8 | 2 | 4th below | 237 | 237 | 0.0e+0 |

and identical again with a note committed at three different positions
(70/70, 62/62, 41/41), which is the case the tool actually runs rather than the
one that is easiest to check. 40,000 draws over the 235-canon space found all
235, none illegal, chi-square **220.0 on 234 df**.

**A canon costs three to four orders of magnitude.** The same line rules with no
second voice at all admit 1,089,690,354,066 melodies of length 14 — a number
that does not depend on the delay, since there is no second voice to be delayed.
The canon admits:

| delay | states | solve | legal canons | self-imitation costs | parallels cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 512 | 5 ms | 69,011,307 | ×15,790 | ×2.83 |
| 2 | 512 | 5 ms | 119,878,543 | ×9,090 | ×3.84 |
| 3 | 4,096 | 36 ms | 269,327,487 | ×4,046 | ×3.33 |
| 4 | 32,768 | 210 ms | 544,055,816 | ×2,003 | ×3.01 |

**And at the fifth, each simultaneity costs almost exactly one bit.** The count
rises with the delay, but only because a longer delay leaves fewer positions
where both voices sound. Fitting a fixed cost and a per-simultaneity one over 24
configurations — lengths 11 to 16 crossed with delays 1 to 4, so two independent
axes, spanning 7 to 15 simultaneities:

    canons = melodies / (1.89 × 2.012 ^ simultaneities)

worst residual **10.6%** on counts running from 1.5 million to 6.9 billion.

The 2.012 has a mechanism rather than being a fitted number. Six of the twelve
semitone classes are consonant — {0, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9} is exactly half of them — so
a simultaneity whose interval is spread evenly across the twelve costs a factor
of two. One bit per simultaneity, and the measurement says 1.008.

**That law is about the fifth, not about canons.** This is the correction I am
gladdest to have made, because the number is clean enough to have published as
general. Refit the same two parameters separately at each of the eight
imitations:

| imitation | cost per simultaneity | fixed cost | worst residual |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| octave below | 1.719 | ×4.83 | 74.7% |
| unison | 1.719 | ×4.83 | 74.7% |
| 3rd below | 1.931 | ×1.67 | 17.1% |
| **5th below** | **2.001** | ×2.02 | **11.2%** |
| 4th below | 2.133 | ×1.47 | 41.3% |
| 7th below | 2.153 | ×1.30 | 56.4% |
| 6th below | 2.198 | ×0.98 | 51.3% |
| 2nd below | 2.237 | ×1.01 | 87.5% |

Only the fifth and the third are described by it. And the framing fails a second
way: if each imitation simply had its own constant, the 30% spread in those
constants would produce a ×23.6 spread in the counts over twelve simultaneities,
where the measured spread is ×2.33. The constants and the fixed costs trade off
against each other, so they are not two independent facts about an imitation.

The octave and unison are the clearest failures and have the clearest reason.
There the follower sings the leader's own pitch classes, so the vertical
interval *is* the melody's interval across d notes — a quantity the melodic
rules have already shaped — and it is no longer spread evenly across the twelve.
The per-simultaneity cost drops to 1.72 accordingly. The law needs the harmonic
and melodic constraints to be roughly independent, and imitating at the octave
is exactly the case where they are not.

**The textbook's fifth is not the roomiest imitation.** Counted at length 14,
delay 2, all rules on:

| imitation | legal canons |
| --- | --- |
| **3rd below** | **247,150,152** |
| octave below | 234,660,242 |
| unison | 234,660,242 |
| 5th below | 119,878,543 |
| 7th below | 115,631,282 |
| 6th below | 106,396,784 |
| 4th below | 105,993,458 |
| 2nd below | 105,962,457 |

The third below leaves more than twice the room the fifth does. (The octave and
the unison agreeing exactly is a check rather than a finding — every interval
here is taken mod 12, so they cannot differ.) The fifth's standing in the
literature is about tonal answer, which this model contains nothing of; it is
not about how much room the interval leaves, and now there is a number saying
so.

**Nothing simple predicts that ordering, and I tried four things.** Consonance
is doing the sorting: turning the cadence rule off changes the spread from ×2.33
to ×2.32, turning parallels off makes it ×2.70, and consonance *alone* still
gives ×2.21. So it is a harmonic effect and not a side-effect of some other
rule. But no first-order harmonic statistic reproduces it:

| predictor | r against log(count) |
| --- | --- |
| is a degree consonant against itself, moved by q | 0.656 |
| fraction of all 64 degree pairs that are consonant | 0.582 |
| the same, weighted by how melodies actually move d notes apart | 0.839 |

Even the weighted version — which is the honest one, built from the empirical
joint distribution of (note, note d earlier) over melodies satisfying the line
rules alone — mispredicts by up to 70% and gets the order wrong in the middle of
the table. Consecutive simultaneities share notes and are not independent, and
the ordering lives in that dependence. I do not have it.

**Rule costs**, at the fifth, delay 2, against 119,878,543 canons: consonance
×1,606, parallels ×3.84, singable line ×3.67, cadence ×2.26, variety ×1.67.
Consonance dominates everything, which is not a surprise; that parallels edges
out the singable-line rule is mildly one, given `species` found the melodic rule
worth more than its other four together.

**The sound is the canon.** With the melody pinned so every pass repeats it, the
leader soloed and the follower soloed and each recorded separately, every note
read back off the audio as the one that was written — 16/16 and 16/16, worst 12
and 6 cents off the candidate grid. And in the dispatch log, which records both
voices at every position whether or not they are audible, the follower is the
leader delayed by two in **42/42** positions. The drawn canon breaks 0 rules
when the melody recovered from it is handed back to the rule definition.

Levels: 0.62 pre-limiter at the defaults, through the smoke gate.

## What went wrong

**A broken RNG in the harness read as a bug in the thing being tested.** The
uniformity check came back at chi-square 1697 on 234 df — decisively not uniform
— and I was ready to go hunting in the sampler, which is the fiddliest code in
the sketch. The sampler was fine. I had written a throwaway LCG in the harness,
`s = (s * 1103515245 + 12345) & 0x7fffffff`, and in JavaScript that multiply
exceeds 2^53 and silently loses precision in exactly the low bits an LCG's
randomness lives in. Swapping in the repo's `rng` gave 220.0 on 234 df with no
other change. `CLAUDE.md` says to take randomness from `@core`; it turns out
that applies to the measuring instrument too, and for a sharper reason than
reproducibility.

**I wrote the notes field before the measurements and most of it was wrong.**
Not wrong in tone — wrong in fact: it claimed the third and the sixth were the
roomiest imitations (the sixth is near the bottom), that the fourth was the
narrowest (it is joint sixth), that a canon costs "about two orders of
magnitude" (three to four), and that the parallels rule bites harder in a canon
than in free counterpoint, which I had not measured at all and still have not.
Writing the prose first is a good way to find out what you think; shipping it
without going back over it against the table is how a sketch acquires a
confident paragraph nobody ever checks.

**Two explanations for the imitation ordering failed before I found the right
question.** I proposed the diagonal (is a degree consonant against itself) and
then the flat pair density, and printed both next to the counts, and neither
correlated. The move that helped was not a third guess but changing the
question: instead of predicting the ordering, ask which *rule* produces it, by
turning each one off and watching the spread. That took three cheap solves and
narrowed it to consonance immediately, at which point the remaining failure is
specific and interesting rather than vague.

## Next

- [ ] Free two-part counterpoint under exactly these rules, so "a canon costs
      ×9,090" can be split into what the second voice costs and what the
      *self*-imitation costs. Right now the comparison is against no second
      voice at all, which overstates the canon-specific part.
- [ ] Why the per-simultaneity law holds at the fifth and third but not the
      octave: the conjecture is that it needs the harmonic and melodic
      constraints to be independent, which is testable by measuring the mutual
      information between the melodic interval and the vertical interval at each
      imitation.
- [ ] Canon by inversion and by augmentation — one line in the spec (the
      follower's pitch array, and its index map) and the classical repertoire of
      canon types opens up. Augmentation breaks the state model, though: the
      follower would be at i/2, which is not a fixed lag.
- [ ] A coda. Real canons need one because the follower's tail has no
      accompaniment, and the tail is audibly the weakest part of what this plays.
- [ ] The count as a *compositional* signal rather than a fact: colour each cell
      by how much fixing it would collapse the space, so the tool shows you
      which decisions are expensive before you make them.
