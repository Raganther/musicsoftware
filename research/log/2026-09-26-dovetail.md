# 2026-09-26 — dovetail: the aggregate is a tiling, and pitch is where it lives

**Sketches touched:** `sketches/dovetail`
**Settings worth keeping:** the defaults — hexachord C (024579), second voice
I, together, spread 1, seed 6, root 3. The demonstration is the `Second voice`
select: `broken` is a form that is not combinatorial, and you hear the two
voices start colliding. `free (from the seed)` asked for I or P is the other
control — a hexachord that simply has no such partner.

## What I tried

Composition tools, the stalest family — last visited 2026-09-21 with
`shorthand`. The idea has been sitting in the sequencing section since `nest`:

> Nest in *pitch* rather than time — the same recursion over an interval gives
> a chord whose notes partition an octave the way these partition a bar.

This repo has tiled **time** four times now — `tiling`, `hocket`, `nest`,
`vuza` — always the same shape of claim: every pulse struck once, no gaps and
no collisions. The same question in pitch already has a name and a literature,
and I had not noticed. A twelve-tone row splits into two hexachords, and the
row is **hexachordally combinatorial** when some transformation of it begins
with the six notes the original ends with. Run the two together and every
half-row is all twelve pitch classes exactly once. That is Schoenberg's device,
and it is a tiling of the aggregate.

## What I measured

Everything is `sketches/dovetail/rows.ts`, run in node before anything sounded.

**Babbitt's hexachord theorem, exhaustively.** A hexachord and its complement
have the same interval vector: **identical for 924 of 924**, every hexachord
there is. Two halves of the aggregate cannot help sounding alike, which is a
fact about complementation rather than about any row.

**Which hexachords are combinatorial, by kind:**

| kind | of 924 |
| --- | --- |
| P | 72 |
| I | 348 |
| R | **924** |
| RI | 216 |
| all four | 48 |

**Retrograde works for every hexachord**, without exception, because the
retrograde of a row starts with the notes the row finished with. That is a fact
about the definition and not about the music, and it is the trap the sketch
walked into (below).

**The 48 are six set classes, and they are the published six.** Found by
search, labelled by prime form:

| | prime form | members of the 924 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | (012345) | 12 |
| B | (023457) | 12 |
| C | (024579) | 12 |
| D | (012678) | 6 |
| E | (014589) | 4 |
| F | (02468T) | 2 |

12 + 12 + 12 + 6 + 4 + 2 = 48, and every count is that set's own transpositional
symmetry — D maps to itself at 6, E at 4, F at 2. Babbitt's six source sets
arrived out of a brute-force search over 924 subsets, which is the known answer
this needed.

**Combinatoriality belongs to the unordered hexachord, so the tune is free.**
Reordering the row inside each half 200 times, for each of the six source sets:
still all-combinatorial **200/200**, and with the identical partner set
**200/200**. The first six notes decide the structure and the melody is yours —
which is what makes it a tool rather than a cage.

**Most rows have no partner at all.** Over all 924 hexachords, counting forms
that dovetail:

| partners | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 12 | 24 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| hexachords | **384** | 480 | 48 | 6 | 4 | 2 |

384 have only the trivial retrograde. The two with 24 are the whole-tone
hexachord and its complement, which is the most symmetric thing in the system.

**And off the sound.** In `alternating` texture the voices hocket, so one note
sounds at a time and twelve consecutive notes are one half-row of each voice.
The tap is armed before the transport starts, so note 0 is note 0:

| hexachord | second voice | distinct pitch classes per aligned twelve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C (024579) | I | **12 12 12 12** |
| C (024579) | P | **12 12 12 12** |
| A (012345) | I | **12 12 12 12** |
| F (02468T) | P | **12 12 12 12** |
| C (024579) | broken | 8 8 8 8 |
| free, from the seed | I | 10 10 10 10 |
| free, from the seed | P | 9 9 9 9 |

**The alignment being special is the part worth checking**, so here is the
count at every offset rather than only the one I wanted:

```
C (024579) I        12.0 11.0 10.0 9.0 8.0 7.0 6.0 7.0 8.0 9.0 10.0 11.0
C (024579) broken    8.0  8.0  9.0 8.0 9.0 8.0 8.0 7.0 8.0 7.0  8.0  7.0
```

A clean V touching twelve exactly once — shifting the window by k swaps k notes
of one half-row for k of the other, and the overlap costs one pitch class per
step until it turns around at 6. The broken row is flat and never reaches
twelve at any offset at all.

Levels: **0.496** at the defaults, **0.700** worst over twelve settings.

## What went wrong

**A fallback made every hexachord look combinatorial.** When the requested
partner kind was not available I wrote `avail.find(k) ?? avail[0]` — and
`avail[0]` is retrograde at t = 0, which *every* row has. So a seeded random
hexachord asked for an inversional partner quietly got the trivial retrograde
instead, dovetailed perfectly, and reported `12 12 12 12`. The sketch was
teaching the opposite of the truth: that combinatoriality is free. The measured
number was correct and the thing it measured was not what the panel said. Now
the requested kind is played whether or not it exists, the aggregate breaks
where it should, and the panel names the partner that does not exist — which
turns the bug into the second control.

**And I nearly shipped a prime form nobody would recognise.** My first
`setClass` took the lexicographically smallest rotation, which is a perfectly
good canonical form and calls (023457) "(0,1,2,3,5,10)". The counts were all
correct — six set classes, 48 hexachords — but the labels could not be checked
against the literature, which is the entire value of finding six. Sorting by
span first and packing left costs four lines and turns a consistent label into
a comparable one.

**A control that cannot fail is not a control**, which is the same lesson from
`dichotic` in a new place: `R` reads 924 of 924 and it means nothing. I nearly
reported it as a result before noticing it is true by definition.

## Next

- [ ] **Trichordal derivation.** A row built from one trichord and its four
      transformations is combinatorial at a finer grain — every *quarter* of it
      is a discrete tetrachord-complete set. The same tiling one level down, and
      `nest`'s recursion is exactly the right shape for it.
- [ ] **All-interval rows**, of which there are famously 3856. A row whose
      eleven intervals are all different is a melody that never repeats a step,
      and `isAllInterval` is already written and unused. Whether any of them are
      also all-combinatorial is a question the two searches answer together.
- [ ] Point `shorthand` at a serial piece. It is built entirely from transforms
      of one row, so a compressor with a composer's vocabulary should find it
      almost free — and a naive statistic should see noise. That is the sharpest
      test of `shorthand` available and it needs no corpus.
- [ ] **Tetrachordal and hexachordal *arrays*** — more than two voices, where
      three rows partition the aggregate between them. That is the direct
      analogue of `nest` going past two voices, and Uspensky has no say here.
- [ ] Say *which* pitch class a broken pair loses, and when. The panel counts
      them; naming them would let you fix a row by ear rather than by search.
- [ ] The interval vector is drawn and never used. Two hexachords with the same
      vector sound alike in a specific measurable way, and `dichotic`'s
      machinery could ask whether a listener hears the complement relation.
- [ ] The honest gap: this proves the partition, not that anyone hears it.
      Same gap `veil`, `dichotic`, `vuza` and `elbows` all left.
