# 2026-08-21 — crossing: the canon that never comes back

**Sketches touched:** `sketches/crossing`
**Settings worth keeping:** the defaults — 3 voices, 26 s sweep, span 5, seed 4
— with `Spread the crossings` at 0, so all three voices pass through the same
tempo at the same instant and the piece has one clear pivot. Two voices with
span 8 is the purest Canon X and the easiest to actually hear trading places.
`Spread the crossings` at 0.6 with five voices is the other thing worth
keeping: the pivots stagger and it stops being an event and becomes a texture.

## What I tried

Sequencing/rhythm, the stalest family (last visited 2026-08-16, continuum).

`convergence` (2026-08-04) is a tempo canon at fixed ratios — 3:4:5 — and its
whole character is that it comes back together on a schedule, every 5.19
seconds, measured. Nancarrow's other trick is the opposite one: Study #21,
*Canon X*, where one voice accelerates continuously while another decelerates,
they cross in the middle, and after that the faster voice is the slower one.
Nothing about it repeats.

The law has to be exponential. A constant *percentage* per second is what reads
as a smooth accelerando, so rate(t) = r0·e^{kt}, and the elapsed event count
integrates to (r0/k)(e^{kt} − 1), which inverts:

    t_n = ln(1 + n·k/r0) / k

Every note is placed from that closed form. Adding an interval to the last note
would accumulate error across a 26-second sweep and put the end somewhere else,
and more to the point the closed form is what makes the whole thing checkable —
a measured onset can be compared against an exact predicted time rather than
against a redraw of the same loop.

## What I measured

Pure sines an octave apart, melody clamped to four scale degrees so the voices
stay in separate frequency bands, 24 s sweep, span 5.

**The known-answer channel first**, before believing anything: do the heard
onsets land where the formula says?

| voice | matched | lag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 (accelerating) | 85 / 96 | 9.9 ± 1.1 ms |
| 1 (steady) | 79 / 86 | 7.9 ± 2.2 ms |
| 2 (decelerating) | 88 / 96 | 6.9 ± 5.4 ms |

That table *is* the confirmation of the exponential law — the closed form is
that law integrated — and it is much stronger than the rate fit below.

**They trade places** at a predicted 12.00 s; measured **11.80 s**.

**The coincidences do not repeat.** 24 coincidences within 30 ms over the
sweep, gaps **0.95 ± 0.66 s** — a spread of 69% of the mean. For any
fixed-ratio canon that spread is exactly zero, by construction. This is the
direct counterpart to convergence's 5.05 s clockwork.

Pre-limiter peak 0.426. Green: 28 sketches + jam, no clipping, clean teardown.

## The prediction I got wrong, and the algebra that explained it

I expected the coincidences to crowd around the crossing — the two rates are
equal there, so surely that is where events line up — and to thin out towards
the ends. The measurement said no: slope −0.049 s per s against distance from
the crossing, r −0.21. No trend, and if anything the wrong sign.

The algebra says why, and it is a nicer fact than the one I predicted. The
outer voices are symmetric, k_a = −k_b, so

    r_a(t)·r_b(t) = r0_a·r0_b·e^{(k_a+k_b)t} = r0_a·r0_b

is **invariant**. The expected rate of coincidences inside a ±d window is
2·d·r_a·r_b, so it is constant across the whole sweep: the voices meet at a
steady average rate but at unpredictable times. That is a Poisson process, and
Poisson has sd equal to the mean.

| | predicted | measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mean gap | 1.30 s | 0.95 s |
| sd / mean | 1.00 | 0.69 |

The rate runs a little high because onset timing error widens the effective
window beyond 30 ms, and the spread runs low because near-coincidences cluster
in pairs. But the shape of the claim survives, and it is a better description
of what Canon X does than "they crowd at the crossing" would have been.

## The estimator that was worse than the thing it estimated

Fitting rate(t) from windowed median intervals gives r 0.88 and 0.92 for the
two sweeping voices — the right sign, the right shape — but |k| comes out
0.049 and 0.050 against a set 0.067, a quarter low, and consistently so for
both.

That is the estimator, not the sketch. About 10% of onsets go missing, and a
missing onset doubles an interval, which moves ln(rate) by 0.69 against a total
sweep of ln(5) = 1.6. I checked the obvious explanation — that misses would
concentrate where a voice is fast and crowded — and it is only weakly true
(93% found while slow against 86% while fast), not enough to account for a
26% shortfall. So I do not know precisely why the fit flattens, and it does not
matter much, because the direct check against the closed form is the same claim
made better.

Worth recording as a general point: I built the weaker measurement first
because it looked more like a physics experiment, and only afterwards noticed
that the ground-truth comparison I had built as a *sanity check* was the
stronger evidence for the same law. The check was the result.

## The recurring failure, day twenty

The known-answer channel paid off immediately and for the third time running.
The first run reported 1 of 96 notes matched — and because that channel exists,
the reading was obviously "the harness is broken" rather than "the sketch is
silent". It was: the sketch loops its sweep, and I read `origin` *after*
listening, so it came back one whole sweep later and filtered every onset out.
Thirty seconds to find, where a fortnight ago it would have been an hour of
adjusting the sketch.

Two measurement traps recurred from earlier days and were caught by inspection
rather than by suffering, which is the first time that has happened:
overlapping notes making an envelope detector re-arm inside them (groove,
yesterday), and a reverb tail with a shape of its own sitting on top of the
thing being measured (bloom, the day before). Both were designed out before the
first run — short pings, `Room` at 0.

The genuinely new one is the 8 ms attack. A 3 ms ramp on a *pure sine* is still
a broadband click, and its splatter arrived in every other voice's band at the
same instant, which had the detector hearing 150 notes where 96 existed. Band
separation is not only about where the steady tone sits; it is about the
transient too.

## Next

- [ ] Non-symmetric sweeps: if k_a ≠ −k_b then r_a·r_b is no longer invariant
      and the coincidence rate should sweep too. That is a sharper test of the
      same algebra and one parameter away.
- [ ] Let the *melody* stretch with the tempo, so an accelerating voice also
      compresses its intervals. Nancarrow's canons are usually rhythmic only;
      the pitch version might be unbearable, which is worth knowing.
- [ ] A countdown to the crossing — `convergence`'s follow-on idea, and it
      applies better here because the arrival is unique rather than periodic.
- [ ] Sweep the tempo along a drawn curve instead of an exponential, so the
      crossings can be composed. The scheduler needs a numerically inverted
      phase function; everything else stays.
- [ ] Two crossings in one piece: sweep down then up, so the voices meet twice
      and the second meeting is at a different tempo.
