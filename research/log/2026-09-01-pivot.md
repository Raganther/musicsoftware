# 2026-09-01 — pivot: where a modulation actually happens

**Sketches touched:** `sketches/pivot`, `src/core/audio.ts`
**Settings worth keeping:** the defaults — C major, +1 fifth, 2 pivot chords,
8 chords each side, 3 modulations, seed 4. Watch the heatmap rather than the
chord row: the ear's turn arrives *after* the purple pivot, on the red chord.
Then set `Pivot chords` to 0 and play the same modulation — the join is
suddenly obvious. Set `Modulate by` to 6 and it tells you no chord belongs to
both keys, so a tritone modulation cannot be pivoted at all.

## What I tried

Composition tools, the stalest family — last visited on 2026-08-27 with
`rhyme`.

The score changes key at the double bar. A listener has no double bar; they
have a running sense of what key they are in, and it turns over when the
evidence does. A **pivot modulation** is built precisely so those two do not
coincide: the pivot chord belongs to both keys, so at the moment the music
crosses over it plays something that cannot distinguish them. The junction is
deliberately inaudible, and the news can only arrive later — with the first
chord the old key cannot explain.

The tool composes that: pick a key, a distance in fifths, and how long to dwell
on the pivot; it finds the shared chords, voice-leads the progression, and
plays it while showing a live 24-key heatmap of what the music sounds like it
is in.

The measurement started as "pivot versus direct", which is only a one-chord
effect. Prolonging the pivot is much better, because it turns a comparison into
a **pair of slopes**: lengthening the pivot area moves the notated junction
earlier and earlier relative to the first foreign chord while leaving the
evidence untouched, so

- the lag behind the **junction** must rise **1:1** with pivot length
- the lag behind the **first foreign chord** must stay **flat at 0**

Slope 1 against slope 0 is far harder to hit by accident than any single
number, and prolonged pivots are ordinary practice rather than a contrivance
invented to make a graph.

Which chord is the first foreign one is computed from pitch classes, not
assumed from the roman numeral, because it depends on the direction of travel.
Sharpward, it is the new V and its raised fourth degree. Flatward, the new
key's V *is* the old tonic and gives nothing away, so the news waits for a
chord carrying the flattened seventh.

## What I measured

Krumhansl-Schmuckler with the published Kessler profiles, unmodified, over
chroma taken from the recorded audio, on a **centred** window — a causal
smoother has a direction built into it and the direction of time is the thing
being measured.

**The shape holds; the precision does not.** Modulating by −2 fifths:

| pivot chords | junction | first foreign | crossing | behind junction | behind foreign |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 12 | 12 | 16.60s | +1.16 | +1.16 |
| 1 | 12 | 13 | 19.84s | +3.75 | +2.75 |
| 2 | 12 | 14 | 20.92s | +4.63 | +2.63 |
| 3 | 12 | 15 | 22.35s | +5.75 | +2.75 |
| 4 | 12 | 16 | 23.43s | +6.63 | +2.63 |

Slopes: **1.29 behind the junction, 0.29 behind the foreign chord.** A second
seed gives **0.69 and −0.31**. Pooled, that is **0.99 against a predicted 1.00**
and **−0.01 against a predicted 0.00** — but with a between-seed spread of about
±0.30 on each, so this is a *directional* result and I am not going to dress it
up as a precise one.

The strongest form of the evidence is within a seed rather than across them.
Once a pivot exists at all, the lag behind the foreign chord holds to **0.12
chords** across pivot lengths 1–4 for seed 4 (2.75, 2.63, 2.75, 2.63) and
**0.04** across lengths 2–4 for seed 17 (0.24, 0.21, 0.25) — while the junction
moved four chords. The thing that is supposed to be flat is very flat; where it
sits differs between seeds.

**Repeatability is 0.000 chords**, which as of `wolf` I know to report as what
it is: the pipeline is deterministic, so a repeat bounds run-to-run noise at
zero and bounds bias at nothing at all. The honest error bar here is the
between-seed spread, which is 0.30, not the repeat.

## The limitation, which is the more useful finding

**The key finder cannot see close modulations.** On music that never modulates
at all, the sticky-crossing rule falsely reports a modulation from C to G at
26.9 s, to F at 22.0 s and to D at 24.9 s. Only C to Bb survives — 416 of 416
frames, mean margin 0.664.

The reason is not subtle once you look at the chroma: six seconds of an ordinary
ii–V–I genuinely contains more of the dominant than of the tonic, so a
chroma-correlation key finder wobbles across that boundary on its own. Over the
whole run it picks Am 215 times, G 87, Dm 68 and C only 46 — the relative minor
and the circle-of-fifths neighbours, which is exactly what Krumhansl-Schmuckler
is known to do on short windows.

So every number above is measured at the one modulation distance the detector
was validated for, and the classic close modulations — the ones a composer
actually writes — could not be measured at all. That is a fact about the method
and not evidence about listeners. It does sit suggestively next to *why* pivots
work, but I am not going to claim a perceptual result from a detector's failure.

## What went wrong

**I validated the wrong quantity first.** The initial check was "does the key
finder name the home key?", which it managed 44% of the time, and I nearly
concluded the whole thing was unmeasurable. But nothing here reads a 24-way
argmax; every measurement is a two-way discriminant — has the target key
overtaken the home key — and that is what needed validating. Lengthening the
window then made the argmax *worse* (17%) and the discriminant better, which is
the clearest possible sign that the two are different questions. **Validate the
quantity the experiment reads, not the one the method is famous for.**

**And then validated the wrong version of the right quantity.** Frame-wise
accuracy still was not it: the experiment reads one number per run, the time of
a crossing that sticks. So the thing to measure is the false-positive rate of
*that rule* on music with no modulation in it — which is what finally gave a
usable answer, and it disqualified three of four targets.

**"Stayed positive for three seconds" is trivially true when there are no
samples in those three seconds.** Every run was reporting a crossing near the
end of its capture until the hold window was required to actually exist.

**A hypothesis I can show was wrong.** Yesterday's `inertia` entry found that
`noiseBuffer()` builds every reverb's impulse response from unseeded
`Math.random()`, so each page load gets a different room. When `pivot`'s key
finder proved unstable between runs I assumed the same cause — a random room
comb-filters the spectrum, and chroma is exactly what that would corrupt. So I
seeded the buffer and re-tapped the signal before the reverb, and the numbers
came back **bit-identical**: 302/416, 320/416, 337/416, 416/416, false positives
at exactly 26.9, 22.0 and 24.9 s. The reverb had nothing to do with it. The
seeding is still worth having and is now in `@core` — it fixes the `inertia`
problem for real — but it did not fix this one, and the earlier variation I
blamed on it was a window-length change I made in the same edit.

## Two things the gate turned up, neither about `pivot`

**Seeding the reverb did not make the suite deterministic, and I should not
have expected it to.** After the `@core` change I ran `npm run smoke` and got
two failures — `arc: silent` and `foreshadow: clipping into the limiter
(1.19)`. Both sketches use `reverb` at mix 0.3, so my change reached them, and
the obvious inference was that I had caused it. The test that settles it is
cheap: if the room is now fixed, a repeat should reproduce the numbers exactly.
It did not — `arc` came back at 0.748 and `foreshadow` at 0.964, and the suite
passed. The sketches carry their own randomness, so a fixed room removes one
source of variance out of several.

**`foreshadow` runs too close to the ceiling.** Across four runs it has read
0.65, 0.93, 0.96 and 1.19 pre-limiter. The 1.19 is a real gate failure and it
predates today — it was 0.926 on the run before the core change and 0.966 two
days ago. The spread is the sketch's own generativity: how many commits happen
to pile onto one bar. Gain 6.0 → 4.5, which puts the loudest thing actually
observed at 0.89. Worth being explicit that no single run bounds the worst case
here; this is scaled against an observed maximum, not a computed one, which is
the same weakness the 10 s smoke window has.

## Next

- [ ] A key finder that can see close modulations. The obvious candidate is
      tracking the *leading tone* rather than the whole profile: what
      distinguishes C from G is one pitch class, and correlating twelve of them
      throws that away. A likelihood ratio on the two candidate keys only.
- [ ] Measure the asymmetry the arithmetic already predicts: sharpward
      modulations should register earlier than flatward ones, because the new V
      is foreign going up and is the old tonic coming down. The sketch draws
      this; nothing has measured it.
- [ ] Pivot on a chord that is diatonic to both but *functionally* different in
      each — ii in one key and vi in the other is a far stronger pivot than a
      shared IV, and the tool currently picks at random among them.
- [ ] Chromatic pivots: the German sixth and the diminished seventh belong to
      several keys at once, which is how nineteenth-century music modulates
      anywhere it likes. One more chord table.
- [ ] `species`'s rules as constraints on this progression, so the voice
      leading is correct rather than merely smooth.
- [ ] Direct modulation by tritone, which the sketch already refuses to pivot,
      is the one case where the ear should turn over instantly. That is a clean
      positive control this run does not have.
